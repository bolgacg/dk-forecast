Can one person forecast Danish weather better than the big public models?

Bolgaç Gülen, October 2026

I built my own weather model for Denmark, DKcast, and tested it once on 2026, ten months it had never seen. It beat ECMWF, DMI, yr.no and every other public model on wind, temperature, sunshine and rain. Fed into a wind power forecast, it beat Energinet's day-ahead forecast too.

1How much better than the public models?

2And against yr.no and DMI?

DKcast had 16 to 28% less error than yr.no's forecast and 24 to 33% less than DMI's HARMONIE on wind, temperature and sunshine, and called rain or no rain 18 to 19% better than both. Each was given its own correction per station first.

yr.no is MET Norway's 1 km post-processed forecast as issued (the MET Nordic archive). It has no sunshine forecast, so sunshine is compared with DMI only. Error is the typical miss at the DMI station, on the same hours for every model.

3How did I do it?

By not choosing a model: DKcast reads all of them at once and learns, station by station, which one to trust in which weather.

  1. Every public model as input. 12 forecasts issued the day before, from ECMWF, DWD, DMI, KNMI, MET Norway, the UK Met Office, NOAA and others, at every DMI weather station.
  2. Real measurements as the answer key. The target is what DMI's stations measured, not a model's replay of the weather, so the model learns the local errors of each site.
  3. A forecast with a range. A small neural network (a distributional regression network, after Rasp and Lerch, 2018) predicts a correction to the models' average and how uncertain it is, trained on March to December 2025.
  4. Tested once. The test script was frozen and published before it ran on 1 January to 18 October 2026, and every public model got the same per-station correction, so the comparison is fair to them.

4The gusts were right, the electricity was not

The worst days were not weather misses.

On Easter Sunday and Monday 2026 the forecast had the wind right. But demand was low on the holiday, the day-ahead price went negative, and wind farms were switched off: eastern Denmark's output fell to about 30% of capacity in the middle of a gale. The weather was right, and the power was wrong. That lesson is now part of the energy model, which allows for wind farms being switched off.
